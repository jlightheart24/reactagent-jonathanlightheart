- In the Utah Risk Factor Study's summer vs. non-summer analysis (2016 data; summer defined as June, July, and August; alpha = 0.05), four line items were found to occur more frequently in summer months, and no line items were marked more frequently in non-summer months than in summer. [utah-risk-factor-study:p4:c0; utah-risk-factor-study:p4:c1]
- Line item 22 (Proper cold holding temperatures) had a summer rate ratio of 1.37 (95% LCL 1.23, 95% UCL 1.52; p < 0.005). [utah-risk-factor-study:p4:c1]
- Line item 33 (Proper cooling methods used; adequate equipment for temperature control) had the highest summer rate ratio at 1.50 (95% LCL 1.22, 95% UCL 1.86; p < 0.005). [utah-risk-factor-study:p4:c1]
- Line item 36 (Thermometers provided & accurate) had a summer rate ratio of 1.23 (95% LCL 1.04, 95% UCL 1.45; p < 0.025). [utah-risk-factor-study:p4:c1]
- Line item 28 (Toxic substances properly identified, stored, and used) had a summer rate ratio of 1.15 (95% LCL 1.02, 95% UCL 1.28; p < 0.025). [utah-risk-factor-study:p4:c1]
- The comparison was framed as a rate-ratio test of individual line item rates between summer and non-summer months using 95% confidence intervals, motivated by anecdotal (not established) evidence of seasonal variation. [utah-risk-factor-study:p3:c2]
- The rate ratios are conditional on a violation already occurring during an inspection ('Given that a violation occurs during an inspection'), so they describe the relative likelihood of a line item being marked, not overall violation rates. [utah-risk-factor-study:p4:c0]
- The study cautions that more data is needed before formulating a rationale for why toxic substance storage (line 28) would be in violation more often in summer, and that the line 36 finding may reflect inspectors examining thermometers more frequently in summer because of increased observations of temperature violations rather than a true increase in the problem. [utah-risk-factor-study:p6:c3]
- A stated limitation is participation: submission of data was voluntary, only seven of Utah's 13 local health departments volunteered data, and data were available for approximately 85% of Utah's population in 2016, with paper-record handling likely limiting broader participation. [utah-risk-factor-study:p4:c2; utah-risk-factor-study:p2:c1]
- Another limitation is that the analysis represents only a single year of inspection data that may not be representative of larger trends, and it looks only at the frequency of violations marked on the form; inadequate information makes it impossible to know the precise reasons a given line item was marked. [utah-risk-factor-study:p5:c2]
- Data-quality limitations also apply: all but two participating departments used inspection forms based on the 2009 Food Code that did not match the 2013 FDA form, so most line numbers had to be translated, and departments' rationales for categorizing establishments by risk were inconsistent, with no two departments categorizing establishments the same way. [utah-risk-factor-study:p5:c1; utah-risk-factor-study:p4:c2; utah-risk-factor-study:p7:c2]
- Interpreting the temperature-related findings, the study notes that kitchen temperatures over 90°F are reportedly not uncommon despite ventilation requirements (citing 6-304.11 of the 2013 FDA Food Code), and that cooling equipment efficiency declines past an ambient temperature limit, increasing the likelihood of temperature-abused food. [utah-risk-factor-study:p6:c2]
- These findings are historical Utah local inspection observations from 2016 (analysis conducted by UDOH with AFDO/FDA support) and are not a statement of current FDA model-code legal requirements. [utah-risk-factor-study:p2:c0; utah-risk-factor-study:p4:c1]
