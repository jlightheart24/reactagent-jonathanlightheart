## Final result

- A nursing home is a facility serving a "highly susceptible population" (HSP) under the Food Code definition, because it provides custodial care/health care/assisted living to persons more likely than the general population to experience foodborne disease, such as older adults or immunocompromised persons. [food-code-2026:p38:c0; food-code-2026:p667:c1]
- Vomiting is a reportable symptom: a food employee who has vomiting must report the information to the person in charge in a manner that allows the person in charge to reduce the risk of foodborne disease transmission, including the date of onset of symptoms (FDA Food Code 2026 § 2-201.11(A)(1)(a)). [food-code-2026:p56:c1]
- Required disposition: because the symptom is not attributed to a noninfectious condition, the person in charge must EXCLUDE the food employee under § 2-201.12(A)(1) ("Except when the symptom is from a noninfectious condition, EXCLUDE a FOOD EMPLOYEE if the FOOD EMPLOYEE is: (1) Symptomatic with vomiting or diarrhea"). [food-code-2026:p59:c0; food-code-2026:p58:c1]
- The exclusion is required in both HSP and non-HSP facilities: Annex 3 Table 1a lists vomiting as "EXCLUDE 2-201.12(A)(1)" for facilities serving an HSP and the same for facilities not serving an HSP; the Annex 3 decision tree likewise directs EXCLUDE per Table 1a for a symptomatic employee in an HSP setting. [food-code-2026:p388:c0; food-code-2026:p385:c0]
- Because the employee has no diagnosis, regulatory authority (RA) approval is not required for return: Table 1a states for vomiting "No if not diagnosed" under "RA Approval Needed to Return to Work?" [food-code-2026:p388:c0]
- Earliest conditions for return (symptomatic, not diagnosed): under § 2-201.13(A)(1) the person in charge may reinstate an employee excluded under 2-201.12(A)(1) only if the employee (a) is asymptomatic for at least 24 hours, or (b) provides written medical documentation from a health practitioner stating the symptom is from a noninfectious condition. Since the scenario supplies no noninfectious diagnosis, the 24-hour asymptomatic pathway is the operative one. [food-code-2026:p61:c0; food-code-2026:p388:c0]
- If norovirus were subsequently diagnosed, the disposition becomes stricter: exclusion under § 2-201.12(A)(2) (vomiting/diarrhea plus norovirus diagnosis), and in a facility serving an HSP the employee "remains excluded" until RA approval under 2-201.13(D) is obtained AND the employee is medically cleared as free of norovirus infection (2-201.13(D)(1)) or more than 48 hours have passed since becoming asymptomatic (2-201.13(D)(2)) — the 24-hour rule alone would not suffice in this nursing home. [food-code-2026:p391:c1; food-code-2026:p63:c1; food-code-2026:p59:c0]
- The establishment must also respond to the event itself: § 2-502.11 requires written procedures for employees responding to vomiting or diarrheal events involving discharge of vomitus onto surfaces, addressing actions to minimize spread of contamination and exposure of employees, consumers, food and surfaces; the Code's rationale notes a real potential for spread of harmful pathogens when an employee vomits in the establishment. [food-code-2026:p72:c0; food-code-2026:p419:c1; food-code-2026:p684:c2]
- Why norovirus transmission is a concern (Bad Bug Book, 2012 reference information, not a regulation): foodborne norovirus illnesses are epidemiologically linked to ready-to-eat foods contaminated by food workers, transmission occurs through the fecal-oral route (or vomit, on occasion) and is often associated with improper sanitation controls; salad ingredients and fruit are among the foods most often implicated, and "any ready-to-eat food that is handled by ill food workers may be contaminated." [bad-bug-book:p170:c0; bad-bug-book:p170:c1; bad-bug-book:p168:c2]
- Norovirus is highly efficient at spreading: the infective dose is estimated as low as 1 to 10 viral particles, particles are excreted at levels as high as 1 x 10^12 per gram of feces by both symptomatic and asymptomatic people, onset is usually 24 to 48 hours after exposure (as early as 12 hours), explosive/projectile vomiting is often the first sign, and about 30% of infected people are asymptomatic "silent shedders" still excreting high virus levels. [bad-bug-book:p169:c0; bad-bug-book:p169:c2]
- The nursing home setting amplifies the risk: the Bad Bug Book states norovirus spreads easily from person to person in crowded living situations including nursing homes, rapid spread of secondary infection is particularly evident in institutions such as nursing homes and hospitals, severity is higher in elderly and immunocompromised persons, alcohol-based hand gels do not protect against norovirus, and the virus may continue to pass in bowel movements even after symptoms resolve. [bad-bug-book:p168:c3; bad-bug-book:p170:c2; bad-bug-book:p169:c2]
- The Food Code's own Annex 3 rationale is consistent: noroviruses are highly contagious with an inoculum of as few as 18 viral particles possibly sufficient to infect, shedding usually begins with onset of symptoms, peaks four days after exposure, and may persist for three weeks after recovery, with peak contagiousness during the acute stage — which is why symptom-based exclusion, not diagnosis, is the trigger, and why restrictions are especially stringent for employees serving highly susceptible populations. [food-code-2026:p374:c0; food-code-2026:p374:c1; food-code-2026:p403:c0]
- Authority caveat: the FDA Food Code 2026 is a model code whose provisions (e.g., 2-201.11, 2-201.12, 2-201.13, 2-502.11) are legally binding only where adopted by a jurisdiction; the Bad Bug Book (2012) is pathogen reference information, not a regulation. [food-code-2026:p59:c0; bad-bug-book:p170:c0]
